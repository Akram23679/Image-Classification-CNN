01 / project brief

Recognise everyday objects from tiny photos.

Image classification is the foundation of computer vision, from photo search to self-driving cars. This project builds a CNN from scratch that sorts 32×32 colour images into 10 classes, with a clean validation strategy and an interactive web app.

86.14%test accuracy
60,000colour images

Project goalsScope
  • Accuracy: train a CNN from scratch that beats 85% on the untouched test set.
  • Generalisation: use augmentation, batch normalisation and dropout to avoid overfitting.
  • Sound evaluation: keep a stratified validation split so the test set is used once.
  • Real use: deploy an app that classifies any uploaded photo.
Improvements over a basic notebookEngineering
  • Fixed random seed and a stratified validation split.
  • Rotation set correctly to ±15° (the old value of 0.10 meant ±36°).
  • Fast tf.data pipeline; EarlyStopping decides when training ends.
  • Callbacks all watch val_loss, so the saved model is the best model.
  • The app handles empty input, any image mode and non-square photos.
02 / data and method

60,000 photos, perfectly balanced.

CIFAR-10 has 6,000 images for each of 10 classes. The official 50,000 training images were split 90/10 with stratification into training and validation sets. The 10,000 test images were held back for one final evaluation.

Preprocessing and augmentationInputs
  • Scaling: pixel values from 0–255 to 0–1; labels one-hot encoded.
  • Augmentation: random horizontal flip, rotation (±15°), zoom (±10%) and translation (±10%), applied only during training.
  • Input pipeline: tf.data with shuffling, batches of 64 and prefetching.
  • Optimiser: Adam, learning rate 0.001, categorical cross-entropy.
  • ReduceLROnPlateau: halves the learning rate after 5 epochs without improvement.
  • EarlyStopping: patience 12, restoring the best weights; up to 100 epochs.
  • Hardware: Google Colab T4 GPU, about 16 seconds per epoch.
03 / model architecture

Three convolution blocks, one dense head.

Each block stacks two 3×3 convolutions with ReLU and batch normalisation, then halves the image with max pooling. Filters double from 32 to 128 while dropout rises from 0.25 to 0.40, so deeper layers learn richer features without overfitting.

Why this designRationale
  • Stacked 3×3 convolutions build up from edges and colours to textures and object parts.
  • Doubling filters per block keeps capacity growing as the image shrinks.
  • Rising dropout (0.25 → 0.30 → 0.40 → 0.50) regularises the deeper, larger layers most.
  • The dense layer holds 78% of the weights; the convolution blocks extract features with under 290K.
04 / training

79 epochs, stopped early at the right moment.

Training and validation curves stay close throughout, ending at 87.2% and 87.0%, so the model is not memorising. The learning rate was halved six times. Validation loss bottomed out at epoch 67, and EarlyStopping restored those weights when training ended at epoch 79.

06 / findings

What the model sees, and what fools it.

Findings come from the notebook's test-set evaluation (classification report and confusion matrix).

86.14%

Strong for a model built from scratch

8,614 of 10,000 test images classified correctly, with no pre-trained weights. Training and validation accuracy finished within 0.3 points of each other.

0.94

Vehicles are easiest

Automobile has the best F1 (0.941), followed by ship (0.927) and truck (0.908). Rigid shapes and consistent backgrounds such as water or road likely make vehicles easier to separate than animals.

137

Dogs get mistaken for cats

The largest single error: 137 dogs predicted as cats (and 60 cats as dogs). At 32×32 pixels, furry four-legged pets look alike. Cat has the lowest F1 of any class (0.740).

1,310

Frog is the fallback guess

The model predicted "frog" 1,310 times for 1,000 frogs. Cats (93), deer (80), birds (67) and dogs (55) were misread as frogs, which is why frog has 97% recall but only 74% precision.

72

Cars and trucks overlap

43 automobiles were called trucks and 29 trucks were called automobiles, the main source of vehicle errors. At 32×32 pixels, the shape cues that separate a car from a truck are easy to lose.

∞

A demo that stays online

07 / limitations and next steps

Where to take the model next.

CIFAR-10 is a benchmark with tiny, centred images. Real photos are larger and messier, so these are the main gaps and the clearest routes to higher accuracy.

Limitations
  • Every image is shrunk to 32×32 pixels, so fine detail in real photos is lost.
  • The model knows only 10 classes and always picks one, even for a person or a plate of food.
  • Similar classes (cat vs dog, car vs truck) remain the main error source.
  • Performance on real-world photos may be lower than on the CIFAR-10 test set.
Suggested next steps
  • Transfer learning with a pre-trained network (e.g. ResNet or EfficientNet) for 93%+ accuracy.
  • Residual connections or a deeper network trained from scratch.
  • Stronger augmentation (Cutout, MixUp) and test-time augmentation.
  • Grad-CAM heatmaps to show which pixels drive each prediction.
